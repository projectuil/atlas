Operating systems manage applications, files, processes, and hardware resources with high precision but possess little understanding of the user's actual objective. Whether the user is investigating a cyberattack, writing a research paper, designing a presentation, or preparing for an examination, the operating system treats these activities as unrelated interactions with individual applications. Consequently, users must continuously translate their intentions into sequences of manual actions across multiple software environments.

**Human Impact**

*   Repetitive manual coordination across applications.
*   Increased cognitive workload.
*   Slower task execution.
*   Fragmented digital workflows.
*   Limited operating system assistance for complex knowledge work.

**Context**

Commonly occurs when:

*   Conducting multidisciplinary research.
*   Developing software systems.
*   Investigating cybersecurity incidents.
*   Managing enterprise projects.
*   Completing academic assignments.

**Evidence / Observation**

Users naturally think in terms of objectives such as "finish my report," "analyze this incident," or "prepare today's presentation." In contrast, operating systems recognize only applications, files, and processes. This mismatch forces users to manually coordinate multiple digital resources to achieve a single goal. The limitation becomes increasingly significant as work grows more knowledge-intensive and spans multiple applications.

**Research Notes**

*   Represents a foundational architectural limitation of current operating systems.
*   Extends previous Human Frictions concerning task context and project management.
*   Establishes a basis for future research into intent-aware operating systems capable of organizing computing environments around user goals rather than applications.
