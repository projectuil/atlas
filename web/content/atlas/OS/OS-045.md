As users gain experience, their workflows naturally evolve. They adopt new tools, develop shortcuts, reorganize projects, and change how they perform recurring tasks. However, operating systems generally remain passive environments that require users to manually reconfigure settings, reorganize workspaces, and optimize their digital environment. Although the system continuously observes user interactions, it rarely transforms these observations into meaningful improvements that evolve alongside the user's workflow.

**Human Impact**

*   Repetitive manual workspace optimization.
*   Inefficient use of accumulated work patterns.
*   Lost opportunities for productivity improvements.
*   Increased effort maintaining personalized environments.
*   Limited long-term evolution of the computing experience.

**Context**

Commonly occurs when:

*   Developing professional expertise.
*   Repeating similar workflows over months or years.
*   Managing recurring projects.
*   Learning new software tools.
*   Transitioning into specialized careers.

**Evidence / Observation**

Experienced users often develop highly consistent workflows through repetition. Despite these stable patterns, operating systems continue treating each interaction as an isolated event instead of recognizing long-term behavioral evolution. As a result, users repeatedly perform optimizations that could potentially be learned and supported by the operating system itself.

**Research Notes**

*   Moves beyond automation toward long-term behavioral adaptation.
*   Highlights the absence of continuous learning in current operating system architecture.
*   Supports future research into self-evolving and behavior-aware operating systems.
