Users frequently mistake familiarity for genuine understanding. After repeatedly encountering a concept, term, or explanation, they may believe they fully understand it simply because it appears recognizable. However, when required to explain the concept, apply it in a new situation, or solve related problems, users often discover that their understanding is incomplete. This difference between recognition and comprehension frequently remains unnoticed until knowledge is tested through practical use.

**Human Impact**

*   Overconfidence in knowledge.
*   Reduced learning effectiveness.
*   Increased cognitive load.
*   Errors during application.
*   Lower decision quality.

**Context**

**Commonly occurs in:**

*   Formal education.
*   Professional training.
*   Technical certification.
*   Self-directed learning.
*   Workplace knowledge transfer.

**Evidence / Observation**

This behaviour is consistently observed throughout learning and professional development. Users often become familiar with terminology, definitions, or repeated explanations and interpret this familiarity as complete understanding. Existing learning systems frequently measure recognition through reading or multiple-choice assessments but provide limited support for distinguishing true conceptual understanding from simple familiarity.

**Research Notes**

*   Focuses on distinguishing recognition from genuine understanding.
*   Highlights limitations of familiarity-based learning.
*   Related to comprehension, knowledge assessment, and long-term learning.
